\begin{abstract}
Construction robots can only work where they can physically reach, yet no existing method quantifies in advance how much of a site a given robot can access. This paper introduces the Robot Inclusivity Index, a closed-loop framework that turns a single three-dimensional point-cloud walkthrough of a construction site into a deployment assessment for a mobile robot described by a compact set of physical parameters. From one scan, the framework generates a robot-specific traversable map that fuses footprint, overhead clearance, step, and slope tolerances, measures connected reachability within a required operating area to produce the index and an accessibility map, and ranks candidate physical site changes by their predicted gain in reachable area. A human-guided relocation interface recomputes the index live as an operator moves obstacles, and a repeat scan verifies the realised improvement. We validate the framework at an active construction site across five staged environment settings using an omnidirectional transport robot and a quadruped. Predicted accessibility maps matched the maps annotated from deployment video on 92.6\% of the labelled area, rising to 97.9\% once annotation boundary effects are discounted, and 58 of 60 test conditions were predicted correctly. In four further cluttered areas, physical adjustments guided by the framework raised the index from as low as 44\% to above 93\%, measured by repeat scans. Because every assessment derives from the same scan, a single site visit can screen an entire fleet before deployment.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
The construction sector has long been one of the least automated of the major industries, and robots are now beginning to move from controlled factories onto construction sites. They already take on a wide range of construction tasks, from concrete printing, bricklaying, and welding through material transport and excavation to painting, surveying, and inspection~\cite{Gharbia2020-ml,Xu2026-cr}. Their arrival is driven less by novelty than by necessity, since construction ranks among the least productive industries~\cite{Rathnayake2023-xt}, and moving robots into these tasks also removes workers from hazardous conditions. A large share of these tasks are performed by mobile robots that must travel across the site and position themselves at the work location. As a result, before a robot can transport a load, inspect a structure, or finish a surface, it must first physically reach the area where the work is to be done.

Construction sites are unstructured, cluttered, and constantly changing~\cite{Xu2026-cr}, and the barriers reported in the literature are dominated by the physical environment rather than by any shortfall in what the robot can do.  Across surveyed studies, limitation in workspace is the single most cited challenge, reported in around 43\% of the papers that document barriers, ahead of structural integrity and the complexity of coordinating robot movement~\cite{Gharbia2020-ml}. Field assessments of active sites reveal several recurring constraints, among them human density, surface unevenness, accessibility, lighting variation, and dynamic clutter, and these constraints are not equal in weight. Accessibility is foundational among them, because the remaining constraints only bind within regions the robot can already reach, and dedicated assessments are emerging for several of them~\cite{zeng2024riilux,borusu2024rilsu}.



Reconfigurable and modular platforms tackle constrained spaces by changing their own geometry, splitting or folding to pass through gaps a fixed body could not~\cite{CONCERT,9817632, prabakaran2017htetro}, and newer navigation stacks add learned perception and online replanning. Both act on the robot or its perception rather than on the site itself, demand added mechanism or computation, and operate only after the robot is already on site.

The idea of robot inclusivity, meaning the extent to which an environment admits a given robot, has been raised in earlier work~\cite{robot_inclusivity_2013,buildings14051193}. The methods proposed so far are largely manual, with a person walking the site and judging suitability by eye, and such assessment neither scales across many floors and fleets of different robots nor forecasts the effect of a proposed change. Closer to an automated measure, an inclusivity-style index has been proposed for illumination conditions~\cite{zeng2024riilux}, and a companion index scores floor surface unevenness~\cite{borusu2024rilsu}. Yet neither produces an accessibility score that accounts for the obstructions that most often stop a mobile robot, namely narrow passages, slopes, steps, and limited overhead clearance, and neither returns concrete changes that would improve the site. Automated pipelines built on building information models can turn a known model into a robot-specific grid and estimate coverage for one platform~\cite{chen2024bimcpp}, yet they presume a curated model rather than a single raw scan of a live construction site. Terrain traversability estimation likewise scores where a robot can drive from sensor data~\cite{papadakis2013terrain}, but it serves the online navigation of a robot already deployed rather than a pre-deployment assessment of the site. More broadly, robotics offers mature methods for covering a region that is already known to be navigable~\cite{gabriely2001spanning,choset1998boustrophedon,galceran2013survey} and for exploring unknown space online~\cite{yamauchi1997frontier,zelinsky1993planning}, but no existing method computes, in advance of deployment, how much of a specific site a specific robot can reach, nor identifies the site changes that would increase that reach.

We introduce the Robot Inclusivity Index (RII), a closed-loop framework that works from a single three-dimensional point-cloud walkthrough of a site. From that one scan the framework quantifies how much of the site a specified robot can reach, visualises the reachable and unreachable regions as an accessibility map, and produces a ranked list of physical changes such as relocating stored objects, widening corridors, or adjusting ramps. Each change carries a predicted gain in reachable area that can then be verified on site. Because the traversable map is generated for each robot from the same scan, a single site visit can screen an entire fleet in advance.

The contributions of this paper are fourfold. First, we formulate a traversable-map representation that is aware of the target robot and fuses slab projection with slope and step tolerances. Second, we introduce a reference-footprint protocol that separates the unreachability imposed by the environment from that imposed by the platform. Third, we develop a human-guided relocation mechanism that recomputes the index live as an operator moves candidate obstacles and reports the as-found and optimised scores side by side. Fourth, we validate the framework through a field study at an active construction site.

\section{Robot Inclusivity for Construction Sites}
\label{sec:method}
% =========================================================================
 
Robot inclusivity shifts the question from whether a robot can cope with a site to whether the site admits the robot. Given a three-dimensional point cloud of a site and a target robot described by the parameter set $(L, W, H, s_{\max}, \theta_{\max}, d)$, namely its length, width, height, maximum climbable step, maximum climbable slope, and drive type, our framework computes the fraction of a required operating area the robot can reach and the site changes that would increase it. The drive type distinguishes differential, holonomic (omnidirectional), car-like, and legged platforms; a legged platform is described by the same parameter set, its stepping and climbing ability entering through the step and slope tolerances.
 
\subsection{Robot Inclusivity Index}
\label{sec:rii_def}
The Robot Inclusivity Index quantifies the inclusivity of a site along the accessibility dimension, expressing as a single value how much of the site a given robot can reach. We formulate the index as a ratio because a ratio is dimensionless and comparable across sites of different sizes, and it reads directly as the share of the intended workspace available to the robot. Let $A_{\mathrm{req}}$ denote the required area, meaning the floor region in which the robot is expected to operate, and let $A_{\mathrm{acc}}$ denote the accessible area, meaning the portion of $A_{\mathrm{req}}$ that the robot can reach under its size, tolerances, and drive constraints. The index is
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{RII} = \frac{A_{\mathrm{acc}}}{A_{\mathrm{req}}} \times 100\%.
\label{eq:riih}
\end{equation}
The value depends jointly on the environment and on the robot, so the same site yields a different index for each platform, and a higher value indicates better deployment readiness for that robot on that site.
 
\subsection{Audit Robot and Data Collection}
\label{sec:capture}
The audit scans are captured by the TEAL audit robot (Fig.~\ref{fig:platform}), a tracked platform we developed for construction floors, where loose debris, uneven terrain, and steep transitions defeat most wheeled bases. A commercial tracked chassis was adapted through in-house mechanical, electrical, and software design: brushless DC motors with Modbus-controlled drivers replace the original drive, an aluminium top plate layers the sensing and compute hardware above a 24\,V LiFePO$_{4}$ power stage, and safety combines a rear hardware emergency stop with a 250\,ms command watchdog. The sensor head pairs an Ouster OS1-128U 3D LiDAR with a VectorNav VN-100 rugged IMU, preferred over the LiDAR's internal IMU for its tolerance of track-induced vibration, alongside a stereo camera and a 2D LiDAR, and an NVIDIA Jetson AGX Orin runs the full ROS~2 stack onboard, supporting teleoperated capture as well as autonomous navigation.

To capture a site, the robot is driven through its representative open areas, passages, and obstacle zones while a tightly coupled LiDAR-inertial SLAM pipeline based on LIO-SAM~\cite{Shan2020-ls} registers the sensor data into a three-dimensional point cloud in which every point is expressed in metres. A handheld unit pairing the same sensor stack with a backpack battery covers areas the tracked platform cannot enter, such as stairwells and narrow corridors. One walkthrough is enough because the cloud records the geometry of the site rather than the experience of any particular robot, so the audit robot need not share any physical property with the robots being assessed, and on an active site the assessment is refreshed by repeating the walkthrough.
 
\subsection{Robot-Specific Traversable Map Generation}
\label{sec:travmap}
We convert the point cloud into a 2D occupancy map that is specific to the target robot. The map must be robot-specific because obstacles are relative to capability: a kerb that stops one platform is climbed by another, and a low duct that blocks a tall robot is passed beneath by a short one. The conversion begins from a floor anchor, with the floor height $z_{f}$ estimated as the 10th percentile of the $z$ values of the cloud, which is robust to stray points below the true floor. The cloud is then sliced into three bands relative to this anchor: points below $z_{f}+s_{\max}$ form the floor slab and model the terrain the robot drives on; points between $z_{f}+s_{\max}$ and $z_{f}+H$ form the obstacle slab, since they are too high to climb over and too low to pass beneath; and points above $z_{f}+H$ are discarded because the robot passes below them, which is how limited overhead clearance enters the map. The obstacle slab is projected onto a horizontal grid with a resolution of 0.05\,m per cell, a cell being marked occupied only if it contains at least three points, which suppresses sensor noise. The floor slab is analysed for local gradients and height discontinuities: cells whose slope exceeds $\theta_{\max}$ or whose upward step exceeds $s_{\max}$ are marked non-traversable, while ramps within tolerance, detected by plane fitting, remain traversable; downward discontinuities are not flagged by this analysis. When the scan spans several storeys, a histogram of $z$ values with 0.10\,m bins reveals floor candidates as prominent peaks, peaks closer than 0.20\,m are merged, and each detected floor is processed into its own map.

Formally, let $c$ range over the cells of the horizontal grid of resolution $\delta = 0.05$\,m, let $n(c)$ count the obstacle-slab points whose horizontal coordinates fall in $c$, and let $\theta(c)$ and $h(c)$ denote the local slope and upward step height estimated from the floor slab at $c$. The occupancy, slope, and step tests combine into a single binary non-traversable map,
\begin{equation}
N(c) = \max\bigl\{\mathbf{1}[n(c) \ge 3],\; \mathbf{1}[\theta(c) > \theta_{\max}],\; \mathbf{1}[h(c) > s_{\max}]\bigr\},
\label{eq:ntrav}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{1}[\cdot]$ is the indicator function, so $N(c)=1$ marks a cell this robot cannot occupy.

The result is a robot-specific obstacle and traversable map, exported as an occupancy image with accompanying metadata, that reflects what this particular robot can and cannot negotiate.
 
\subsection{Accessibility Analysis}
\label{sec:rii}
The required area is drawn by the user or imported from the site plan.
% TODO state how the required area and the entry point were fixed in the
% experiments (e.g. traced before any analysis was run; entry at the area
% doorway) and that both were identical across robots on the same scan
Free space alone is not accessibility, since a pocket of free floor contributes nothing if the robot cannot travel to it, and we therefore measure connected reachability from the robot entry point rather than raw free area. Reachable space is extracted by inflating obstacles with the robot footprint and collecting the free cells connected to the robot entry point, with the rectangular footprint tested at $K$ discrete headings to ensure that a robot fitting through a passage in one orientation is not rejected. The drive type sets the turning behaviour these tests must respect, since a holonomic platform can translate in any direction, a differential platform can rotate in place but advances only along its heading, and a car-like platform requires clearance for its turning arc. We encode these turning constraints through the footprint inflation applied during reachability extraction, which converts manoeuvrability limits into the same occupancy representation as the other spatial parameters. Formally, let $B_{k}(c)$ denote the cells swept by the $L \times W$ footprint rectangle centred at $c$ with heading $\varphi_{k} = (k-1)\pi/K$, enlarged according to the drive type $d$; headings over $[0, \pi)$ suffice because the rectangular footprint is invariant under a half turn, and cells outside the mapped grid count as non-traversable.
% TODO state the value of K used in the experiments
A cell is free when the footprint fits there at some heading,
\begin{equation}
F(c) = \max_{1 \le k \le K}\; \min_{c' \in B_{k}(c)} \bigl(1 - N(c')\bigr),
\label{eq:inflate}
\end{equation}
and the reachable map $R$ is the connected component of $F$ containing the entry cell: $R(c)=1$ exactly when an 8-connected path of free cells joins the entry cell $c_{0}$ to $c$. Writing $Q(c)=1$ for the cells inside the required area, the areas of~\eqref{eq:riih} follow as
\begin{equation}
A_{\mathrm{acc}} = \delta^{2} \sum_{c} R(c)\,Q(c), \qquad
A_{\mathrm{req}} = \delta^{2} \sum_{c} Q(c).
\label{eq:areas}
\end{equation}
The index is reported together with the accessibility map in the final assessment.
 
To separate the influence of the environment from that of the platform, we run the analysis twice: once with a small reference footprint that represents a practical lower bound on ground robot size, and once with the actual target robot. Shrinking the footprint shrinks every $B_{k}(c)$ and can only enlarge $F$ and $R$, so, writing $\mathrm{RII}_{0}$ for the index of the reference run, $\mathrm{RII} \le \mathrm{RII}_{0}$ and the unreachable share of the required area decomposes as
\begin{equation}
100\% - \mathrm{RII} = \underbrace{\bigl(\mathrm{RII}_{0} - \mathrm{RII}\bigr)}_{\text{platform-imposed}} + \underbrace{\bigl(100\% - \mathrm{RII}_{0}\bigr)}_{\text{site-imposed}}.
\label{eq:decomp}
\end{equation}
The first term is recovered only by choosing a more capable robot, the second only by changing the site.
 
\subsection{Barrier Diagnosis and Improvement Ranking}
\label{sec:barriers}
A score states how inclusive the site is but not what to change, and the framework therefore explains its own result. Cells inside the required area that remain unreachable are clustered into barrier regions, and each region is attributed to a geometric cause, for example a passage narrower than the footprint, a step above tolerance, a slope above tolerance, or a freestanding obstacle blocking a corridor. For barriers caused by freestanding obstacles, the algorithm simulates the removal of the blocking obstacle cluster, recomputes the map, and records the resulting change in the index. Formally, write $\mathrm{RII}[N]$ for the index obtained by carrying a modified map through \eqref{eq:inflate}, \eqref{eq:areas}, and \eqref{eq:riih}, and let $\Omega_{j}(c)=1$ on the cells that are non-traversable only through the occupancy term of \eqref{eq:ntrav} and are attributed to the $j$-th movable obstacle. The predicted gain of removing obstacle $j$ and the optimised score from removing all candidates together are
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
g_{j} &= \mathrm{RII}\bigl[(1-\Omega_{j})N\bigr] - \mathrm{RII}[N],\\
\mathrm{RII}^{\ast} &= \mathrm{RII}\Bigl[N \prod\nolimits_{j}(1-\Omega_{j})\Bigr],
\end{split}
\label{eq:gain}
\end{equation}
with the products taken per cell; because removals interact, the individual gains $g_{j}$ need not sum to $\mathrm{RII}^{\ast} - \mathrm{RII}[N]$. The optimised score $\mathrm{RII}^{\ast}$ is reported alongside the as-found score, and the pair bounds the improvement achievable through site preparation alone. Candidate improvements are then ranked by $g_{j}$ into a prioritised list of actions; whether an obstacle can actually be moved is judged by the operator in the relocation stage that follows.
 
\subsection{Human-Guided Relocation and Closed-Loop Verification}
\label{sec:relocation}
The final stage places a human in the loop to turn the ranked list into a workable plan, since an operator knows constraints the algorithm cannot see, such as which obstacles can actually be moved and where materials are allowed to be stored. The interface overlays the detected obstacle regions on the accessibility map. The operator selects an obstacle they know to be movable and drags it to a proposed location; the traversable map and the index recompute live at interactive rates,

and the display keeps the baseline score, the current score, and the difference between them visible throughout. The operator iterates until the target level of accessibility is reached, and the resulting arrangement becomes a relocation plan for the site team. Predicted gains remain simulations until the site itself changes. After the physical changes are applied on site, the audit robot repeats the scan and the analysis, and the comparison between the predicted and the measured index closes the loop.
 
\subsection{Validation Test Cases}
\label{sec:testcases}
The index is a prediction, and a prediction earns trust only when it matches what a physical robot experiences on the ground. We therefore validate the framework through controlled test cases, each staging one geometric constraint on deployment. The cases span the parameter set of Section~\ref{sec:method}, together with two platform properties it does not model explicitly, ground clearance and wheel width. Each case is staged in conditions on both sides of the relevant capability limit, at least one within the capability of the robot and one beyond it, and a case is counted as correct when the prediction and the observed behaviour agree; the cases are exercised on the field site in Section~\ref{sec:results}.

\section{Experiments and Results}
\label{sec:results}

We evaluate the framework at an active construction site and organise the experiments around three questions: whether the predicted accessibility maps match the behaviour of physical robots, whether the index separates platforms of different capability on the same site, and whether physical adjustments guided by the framework's barrier diagnosis produce measured gains in accessibility.

\subsection{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}
We validated the framework at the New Science Centre construction site in Singapore, an active project whose cluttered and changing conditions match the environments the method targets. Five environment settings were selected across the site, and each setting stages one or more of the validation test cases of Section~\ref{sec:testcases}. Table~\ref{tab:settings} lists the validation test cases and their staged conditions; the items staged in each setting are described below. The settings span three zones of the site: Settings 1 and 3 in Zone 3, Settings 2 and 4 in Zone 2, and Setting 5 in Zone 4.

With the Movilo, Setting~1 staged corridor widths, safety cones, temporary partitions, material stacks, and large materials. Setting~2 added denser floor obstacles, including consumables, wood offcuts, rebar, and a toolbox, together with narrow gaps and floor depressions. Setting~3 ran a reduced set of four conditions because planned items were unavailable on the day. The comparison was further quantified at pixel level for every setting. The behaviour observed in the deployment video was annotated onto a copy of the occupancy map at its 0.05\,m resolution, marking the regions the robot reached as accessible and each obstacle, together with a one-to-two-cell safety buffer, as unreachable, in the same palette used by the predicted map. Because both maps are rendered from the same occupancy grid they should align pixel for pixel; we verified this by cross-correlating the obstacle masks rather than assuming it, a check that caught and corrected a four-pixel offset in the Setting~4 annotation. Agreement therefore reduces to comparing labels at each location.

We report the label similarity, the fraction of identically labelled pixels over the union of pixels labelled in either map, a denominator that penalises area coloured in only one of the two maps. Alongside it we report the colour agreement, the same fraction restricted to pixels labelled in both maps, and a tolerant variant of the label similarity that discounts pixels lying within three pixels of the other map's labelled region, which absorbs the annotation boundary band discussed below. Settings 1 to 3 were assessed with the Movilo and Settings 4 and 5 with the GO2, so the comparison covers both an omnidirectional and a legged platform.

Table~\ref{tab:similarity} summarises the outcome. The predicted and annotated maps carry the same label on 92.6\% of the union area overall, with per-setting values between 90.2\% and 94.1\%, and wherever both maps commit to a label they agree on 98.7\% of pixels. The gap between the two figures is dominated by a thin band at region boundaries rather than by disagreement about accessibility, visible as the red outlines in the disagreement panels of Figs.~\ref{fig:heatmaps_movilo} and~\ref{fig:heatmaps_go2}. Most of this band is the safety buffer the annotation deliberately draws around obstacles, a property of the protocol rather than a prediction error, and per setting 80--100\% of the pixels labelled only in the annotated map lie within three pixels of the predicted region boundary. Discounting the band raises the overall agreement to 97.9\%, with the three-pixel tolerance sitting at the observed plateau of the similarity-versus-tolerance curve rather than being an arbitrary choice.

Three further measures complete the picture. At the level of staged conditions, 58 of the 60 conducted conditions were predicted correctly, a 96.7\% match rate with a Wilson 95\% confidence interval of 88.6--99.1\%, split 26 of 27 for the Movilo and 32 of 33 for the GO2; both misses involved small objects that the point-cloud noise filtering removes. At the level of the index, the RII computed from the predicted map exceeded the value computed from the annotated map in every setting, by 1.4 to 8.1 percentage points and 4.6 pooled, as listed in Table~\ref{tab:similarity}; part of this offset is definitional, since the annotation counts its safety buffer as unreachable, so these figures bound the index error from above. Finally, because the accessible class dominates every map, we also report chance-corrected agreement: pooled Cohen's kappa is 0.62, a level depressed by class imbalance even though the residual disagreement is confined to the boundary band, while the accessible-class intersection over union of 95--98\% and accessible-label precision of 96.4--99.9\% show the labels are recovered reliably. The residual errors are asymmetric, with area wrongly marked accessible totalling 21.5\,m$^2$ across the five settings, eleven times the area wrongly marked unreachable. This asymmetry is acceptable for the intended use: the index is a site-preparation screening tool rather than a safety clearance, responsibility for small objects at deployment time remains with the robot's onboard perception, and the upper-bound reading of the index makes the residual optimism explicit.

\subsection{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
Taken together, the experiments answer the three questions in order. Across the five settings the maps the framework predicted matched the behaviour annotated from video on 92.6\% of the labelled area, on 97.9\% once the annotation boundary band is set aside, and on 58 of 60 conducted conditions, so the accessibility maps can be read as faithful forecasts of where a platform will and will not operate.

These fourteen route-level discrepancies trace to three mechanisms: the filtering of small floor-level objects from the cloud, footprint clearance not being enforced between pairs of obstacles, and negative obstacles such as floor depressions, which the current floor-slab analysis does not flag. Each maps to a concrete pipeline improvement, namely platform-aware filtering thresholds, footprint enforcement between obstacle pairs, and negative-obstacle detection from the ground plane. The direction of the first mechanism is platform-dependent, optimistic for the wheeled platform and conservative for the quadruped. That the errors follow each platform's own capabilities supports assessing a fleet from a single scan, while showing that footprint and climbing tolerances alone do not fully capture how a legged platform negotiates small obstacles. The consistently optimistic index bias argues for reading a reported RII as an upper bound on deployment readiness.

The adjustment tests show that the diagnosed barriers are actionable: relocating movable obstacles raised the index in every area, by 15.6 to 52.1 percentage points, and the near-identical response of the two platforms assessed on the same scans indicates that one round of site preparation can serve several robots at once.

Three limitations remain. First, each assessment is a snapshot of a changing site. Second, all settings come from a single project, with four relocation tests forming a small sample. Third, the adjusted areas were verified by rescans but not yet re-walked by the physical robots, and the gains predicted in the relocation interface have not been compared with the measured ones, so the predicted-versus-realised comparison remains to be reported; the reference-footprint separation of~\eqref{eq:decomp} was likewise not exercised in this study. Broader validation across sites and larger intervention studies remain for future work.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
This paper introduced the Robot Inclusivity Index, a closed-loop framework that turns a single point-cloud walkthrough of a construction site into a deployment assessment for a mobile robot described by a compact parameter set, producing a robot-specific traversable map, an accessibility map, a single-figure index, and a ranked list of site changes with predicted gains. In a field study at an active construction site, the accessibility maps predicted for an omnidirectional and a legged platform matched the behaviour annotated from deployment video on 92.6\% of the labelled area, rising to 97.9\% once annotation boundary effects are discounted, and 58 of 60 test conditions were predicted correctly. Physically adjusting four further cluttered areas guided by the framework's barrier diagnosis raised their indices from as low as 44\% to above 93\%, a mean gain of 37 percentage points measured by repeat scans. Future work will verify the adjusted areas through robot re-deployment, extend validation to additional sites and larger intervention studies, and broaden the index beyond accessibility to the other dimensions of robot inclusivity.
