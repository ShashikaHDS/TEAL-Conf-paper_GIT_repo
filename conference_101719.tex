\begin{abstract}
Construction robots can only work where they can physically reach, yet no existing method quantifies in advance how much of a site a given robot can access. This paper introduces the Robot Inclusivity Index (RII), a framework that turns a single three-dimensional point-cloud walkthrough of a construction site into a deployment assessment for a mobile robot described by a compact parameter set. From one scan, the framework generates a robot-specific traversable map that fuses footprint, overhead clearance, step, and slope tolerances, measures connected reachability within a required operating area to produce the index and an accessibility map, and ranks candidate physical site changes by their predicted gain in reachable area. A human-guided relocation interface turns the ranking into a site-preparation plan whose effect a repeat scan verifies. We validate the framework at an active construction site across five staged environment settings using an omnidirectional transport robot and a quadruped robot. Predicted accessibility maps matched the maps annotated from deployment video on 92.6\% of the labelled area, rising to 97.9\% once annotation boundary effects are discounted, and 58 of 60 test conditions were predicted correctly. In four further cluttered areas, physical adjustments guided by the framework raised the index from as low as 44\% to above 93\%, measured by repeat scans. Because every assessment derives from the same scan, a single site visit can screen an entire fleet before deployment.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Robots are moving from controlled factories onto construction sites and already take on tasks from concrete printing, bricklaying, and welding through material transport and excavation to painting, surveying, and inspection~\cite{Gharbia2020-ml,Xu2026-cr}. Their arrival is driven by necessity, since construction ranks among the least productive industries~\cite{Rathnayake2023-xt}, and robots also remove workers from hazardous conditions. A large share of these tasks fall to mobile robots, and before a robot can transport a load, inspect a structure, or finish a surface, it must first physically reach the area where the work is to be done.

Construction sites are unstructured, cluttered, and constantly changing~\cite{Xu2026-cr}, and reported barriers are dominated by the physical environment rather than by robot capability, with workspace limitation the single most cited challenge, appearing in around 43\% of the papers documenting barriers, ahead of structural integrity and coordination complexity~\cite{Gharbia2020-ml}. Field assessments of active sites reveal recurring constraints such as human density, surface unevenness, accessibility, lighting variation, and dynamic clutter, and accessibility is foundational among them because the remaining constraints only bind within regions the robot can already reach, while dedicated assessments are emerging for several of the others~\cite{zeng2024riilux,borusu2024rilsu}.

Reconfigurable and modular platforms tackle constrained spaces by changing their own geometry, splitting or folding to pass through gaps a fixed body could not~\cite{CONCERT,9817632,sachinthana2025energy}, and newer navigation stacks add learned perception and online replanning. Both act on the robot or its perception rather than on the site itself, demand added mechanism or computation, and operate only after the robot is already on site.

Robot inclusivity, the extent to which an environment admits a given robot, has been raised in earlier work~\cite{robot_inclusivity_2013,buildings14051193}, but the methods proposed so far are largely manual, with a person walking the site and judging suitability by eye, and they neither scale across floors and fleets nor forecast the effect of a proposed change. Closer to an automated measure, inclusivity indexes have been proposed for illumination conditions~\cite{zeng2024riilux} and floor surface unevenness~\cite{borusu2024rilsu}. Yet neither produces an accessibility score covering the obstructions that most often stop a mobile robot, namely narrow passages, slopes, steps, and limited overhead clearance, nor returns concrete changes that would improve the site. Pipelines built on building information models can turn a known model into a robot-specific grid and estimate coverage~\cite{chen2024bimcpp}, yet they presume a curated model rather than a raw scan of a live site, and terrain traversability estimation scores where a robot can drive from sensor data~\cite{papadakis2013terrain} but serves online navigation after deployment rather than pre-deployment assessment. More broadly, mature methods exist for covering known navigable regions~\cite{gabriely2001spanning,choset1998boustrophedon,galceran2013survey} and for exploring unknown space online~\cite{yamauchi1997frontier,zelinsky1993planning}, but no existing method computes, in advance of deployment, how much of a specific site a specific robot can reach, nor identifies the site changes that would increase that reach.

We introduce the Robot Inclusivity Index (RII), a framework that works from a single three-dimensional point-cloud walkthrough of a site. From that one scan it quantifies how much of the site a specified robot can reach, visualises the result as an accessibility map, and produces a ranked list of physical changes such as relocating stored objects, widening corridors, or adjusting ramps, each carrying a predicted gain that can be verified on site. Because the traversable map is generated per robot from the same scan, a single visit can screen an entire fleet in advance.

Our contributions are a robot-aware traversable-map representation fusing slab projection with slope and step tolerances, a reference-footprint protocol separating environment-imposed from platform-imposed unreachability, a human-guided relocation mechanism that recomputes the index live and reports the as-found and optimised scores side by side, and a validating field study at an active construction site.

\section{Robot Inclusivity for Construction Sites}
\label{sec:method}
% =========================================================================
 
Robot inclusivity shifts the question from whether a robot can cope with a site to whether the site admits the robot. Given a three-dimensional point cloud of a site and a target robot described by the parameter set $(L, W, H, s_{\max}, \theta_{\max}, d)$, namely its length, width, height, maximum climbable step, maximum climbable slope, and drive type, our framework computes the fraction of a required operating area the robot can reach and the site changes that would increase it. The drive type distinguishes differential, holonomic (omnidirectional), car-like, and legged platforms, with a legged platform described by the same parameters through its step and slope tolerances.
 
\subsection{Robot Inclusivity Index}
\label{sec:rii_def}
The Robot Inclusivity Index quantifies the inclusivity of a site along the accessibility dimension, expressing as a single value how much of the site a given robot can reach. The index is a ratio, so it is dimensionless, comparable across sites of different sizes, and read directly as the share of the intended workspace available to the robot. Let $A_{\mathrm{req}}$ denote the required area, meaning the floor region in which the robot is expected to operate, and let $A_{\mathrm{acc}}$ denote the accessible area, meaning the portion of $A_{\mathrm{req}}$ that the robot can reach under its size, tolerances, and drive constraints. The index is
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{RII} = \frac{A_{\mathrm{acc}}}{A_{\mathrm{req}}} \times 100\%.
\label{eq:riih}
\end{equation}
The value depends jointly on the environment and on the robot, so the same site yields a different index for each platform, and a higher value indicates better deployment readiness for that robot on that site.
 
\subsection{Audit Robot and Data Collection}
\label{sec:capture}
The audit scans are captured by the TEAL audit robot (Fig.~\ref{fig:platform}), a tracked platform we developed for construction floors, where loose debris, uneven terrain, and steep transitions defeat most wheeled bases. A commercial tracked chassis was adapted through in-house mechanical, electrical, and software design, in which brushless DC motors with Modbus-controlled drivers replace the original drive, an aluminium top plate layers the sensing and compute hardware above a 24\,V LiFePO$_{4}$ power stage, and safety combines a rear hardware emergency stop with a 250\,ms command watchdog. The sensor head pairs an Ouster OS1-128U 3D LiDAR with a VectorNav VN-100 rugged IMU, chosen for its tolerance of track-induced vibration, alongside a stereo camera and a 2D LiDAR, and an onboard NVIDIA Jetson AGX Orin runs the full ROS~2 stack for teleoperated capture and autonomous navigation.

To capture a site, the robot is driven through its representative open areas, passages, and obstacle zones while a tightly coupled LiDAR-inertial SLAM pipeline based on LIO-SAM~\cite{Shan2020-ls} registers the sensor data into a metric three-dimensional point cloud. A handheld unit pairing the same sensor stack with a backpack battery covers areas the tracked platform cannot enter, such as stairwells and narrow corridors. One walkthrough suffices because the cloud records site geometry rather than any robot's experience, so the audit robot need not resemble the robots being assessed, and on an active site the assessment is refreshed by rescanning.
 
\subsection{Robot-Specific Traversable Map Generation}
\label{sec:travmap}
We convert the point cloud into a 2D occupancy map that is specific to the target robot, because obstacles are relative to capability, since a kerb that stops one platform is climbed by another and a low duct that blocks a tall robot is passed beneath by a short one. The conversion begins from a floor anchor, with the floor height $z_{f}$ estimated as the 10th percentile of the $z$ values of the cloud, which is robust to stray points below the true floor. The cloud is then sliced into three bands relative to this anchor. Points below $z_{f}+s_{\max}$ form the floor slab and model the terrain the robot drives on, points between $z_{f}+s_{\max}$ and $z_{f}+H$ form the obstacle slab, since they are too high to climb over and too low to pass beneath, and points above $z_{f}+H$ are discarded because the robot passes below them, which is how limited overhead clearance enters the map. The obstacle slab is projected onto a horizontal grid with a resolution of 0.05\,m per cell, a cell being marked occupied only if it contains at least three points, which suppresses sensor noise. The floor slab is analysed for local gradients and height discontinuities, and cells whose slope exceeds $\theta_{\max}$ or whose upward step exceeds $s_{\max}$ are marked non-traversable, while ramps within tolerance, detected by plane fitting, remain traversable and downward discontinuities are not flagged. When the scan spans several storeys, a histogram of $z$ values with 0.10\,m bins reveals floor candidates as prominent peaks, peaks closer than 0.20\,m are merged, and each detected floor is processed into its own map.

Formally, let $c$ range over the cells of the horizontal grid of resolution $\delta = 0.05$\,m, let $n(c)$ count the obstacle-slab points whose horizontal coordinates fall in $c$, and let $\theta(c)$ and $h(c)$ denote the local slope and upward step height estimated from the floor slab at $c$. The occupancy, slope, and step tests combine into a single binary non-traversable map,
\begin{equation}
N(c) = \max\bigl\{\mathbf{1}[n(c) \ge 3],\; \mathbf{1}[\theta(c) > \theta_{\max}],\; \mathbf{1}[h(c) > s_{\max}]\bigr\},
\label{eq:ntrav}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{1}[\cdot]$ is the indicator function, so $N(c)=1$ marks a cell this robot cannot occupy.

\subsection{Accessibility Analysis}
\label{sec:rii}
The required area is drawn by the user or imported from the site plan.
% TODO state how the required area and the entry point were fixed in the
% experiments (e.g. traced before any analysis was run; entry at the area
% doorway) and that both were identical across robots on the same scan
Free space alone is not accessibility, since a pocket of free floor contributes nothing if the robot cannot travel to it, so we measure connected reachability from the entry point rather than raw free area, inflating obstacles with the rectangular footprint tested at $K$ discrete headings so that a robot fitting through a passage in one orientation is not rejected. The drive type sets the turning behaviour these tests must respect, since a holonomic platform can translate in any direction, a differential platform can rotate in place but advances only along its heading, and a car-like platform requires clearance for its turning arc, and these constraints enter through the footprint inflation, which converts manoeuvrability limits into the same occupancy representation as the other spatial parameters. Formally, let $B_{k}(c)$ denote the cells swept by the $L \times W$ footprint rectangle centred at $c$ with heading $\varphi_{k} = (k-1)\pi/K$, enlarged according to the drive type $d$; headings over $[0, \pi)$ suffice because the rectangular footprint is invariant under a half turn, and cells outside the mapped grid count as non-traversable.
% TODO state the value of K used in the experiments
A cell is free when the footprint fits there at some heading,
\begin{equation}
F(c) = \max_{1 \le k \le K}\; \min_{c' \in B_{k}(c)} \bigl(1 - N(c')\bigr),
\label{eq:inflate}
\end{equation}
and the reachable map $R$ is the connected component of $F$ containing the entry cell, so $R(c)=1$ exactly when an 8-connected path of free cells joins the entry cell $c_{0}$ to $c$. Writing $Q(c)=1$ for the cells inside the required area, the areas of~\eqref{eq:riih} follow as
\begin{equation}
A_{\mathrm{acc}} = \delta^{2} \sum_{c} R(c)\,Q(c), \qquad
A_{\mathrm{req}} = \delta^{2} \sum_{c} Q(c).
\label{eq:areas}
\end{equation}

To separate the influence of the environment from that of the platform, we run the analysis twice, first with a small reference footprint that represents a practical lower bound on ground robot size and then with the actual target robot. Shrinking the footprint shrinks every $B_{k}(c)$ and can only enlarge $F$ and $R$, so, writing $\mathrm{RII}_{0}$ for the index of the reference run, $\mathrm{RII} \le \mathrm{RII}_{0}$ and the unreachable share of the required area decomposes as
\begin{equation}
100\% - \mathrm{RII} = \underbrace{\bigl(\mathrm{RII}_{0} - \mathrm{RII}\bigr)}_{\text{platform-imposed}} + \underbrace{\bigl(100\% - \mathrm{RII}_{0}\bigr)}_{\text{site-imposed}}.
\label{eq:decomp}
\end{equation}
The first term is recovered only by choosing a more capable robot, the second only by changing the site.
 
\subsection{Barrier Diagnosis and Improvement Ranking}
\label{sec:barriers}
A score states how inclusive the site is but not what to change, so unreachable cells inside the required area are clustered into barrier regions, each attributed to a geometric cause, for example a narrow passage, an excessive step or slope, or a freestanding obstacle blocking a corridor. For freestanding obstacles, the algorithm simulates removing the blocking cluster, recomputes the map, and records the resulting change in the index. Formally, write $\mathrm{RII}[N]$ for the index obtained by carrying a modified map through \eqref{eq:inflate}, \eqref{eq:areas}, and \eqref{eq:riih}, and let $\Omega_{j}(c)=1$ on the cells that are non-traversable only through the occupancy term of \eqref{eq:ntrav} and are attributed to the $j$-th movable obstacle. The predicted gain of removing obstacle $j$ and the optimised score from removing all candidates together are
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
g_{j} &= \mathrm{RII}\bigl[(1-\Omega_{j})N\bigr] - \mathrm{RII}[N],\\
\mathrm{RII}^{\ast} &= \mathrm{RII}\Bigl[N \prod\nolimits_{j}(1-\Omega_{j})\Bigr],
\end{split}
\label{eq:gain}
\end{equation}
with the products taken per cell; because removals interact, the individual gains $g_{j}$ need not sum to $\mathrm{RII}^{\ast} - \mathrm{RII}[N]$. The optimised score $\mathrm{RII}^{\ast}$ is reported alongside the as-found score, and the pair bounds the improvement achievable through site preparation alone. Candidate improvements are then ranked by $g_{j}$ into a prioritised list of actions; whether an obstacle can actually be moved is judged by the operator in the relocation stage that follows.
 
\subsection{Human-Guided Relocation and Rescan Verification}
\label{sec:relocation}
The final stage places a human in the loop, since an operator knows constraints the algorithm cannot see, such as which obstacles can actually be moved and where materials may be stored. The interface overlays the detected obstacle regions on the accessibility map, and the operator selects an obstacle they know to be movable and drags it to a proposed location, whereupon the traversable map and the index recompute live at interactive rates

while the display keeps the baseline score, the current score, and their difference visible. The operator iterates until the target level of accessibility is reached, and the resulting arrangement becomes a relocation plan for the site team. After the physical changes are applied on site, the audit robot repeats the scan and the analysis, and the comparison between the predicted and the measured index completes the verification.
 
\subsection{Validation Test Cases}
\label{sec:testcases}
A prediction earns trust only when it matches what a physical robot experiences on the ground, and we therefore validate the framework through controlled test cases, each staging one geometric constraint on deployment. The cases span the parameter set of Section~\ref{sec:method}, together with two platform properties it does not model explicitly, ground clearance and wheel width. Each case is staged in conditions on both sides of the relevant capability limit, at least one within the capability of the robot and one beyond it, and a case is counted as correct when the prediction and the observed behaviour agree; the cases are exercised on the field site in Section~\ref{sec:results}.

The comparison was further quantified at pixel level for every setting. The behaviour observed in the deployment video was annotated onto a copy of the occupancy map at its 0.05\,m resolution, marking the regions the robot reached as accessible and each obstacle, together with a one-to-two-cell safety buffer, as unreachable, in the same palette used by the predicted map. Because both maps are rendered from the same occupancy grid they should align pixel for pixel, which we verified by cross-correlating the obstacle masks, a check that caught and corrected a four-pixel offset in the Setting~4 annotation, and agreement therefore reduces to comparing labels at each location.

We report the label similarity, the fraction of identically labelled pixels over the union of labelled pixels, which penalises area coloured in only one map, together with the colour agreement, the same fraction restricted to pixels labelled in both maps, and a tolerant variant discounting pixels within three pixels of the other map's labelled region, which absorbs the boundary band discussed below.

Table~\ref{tab:similarity} summarises the outcome. The predicted and annotated maps carry the same label on 92.6\% of the union area overall, with per-setting values between 90.2\% and 94.1\%, and wherever both maps commit to a label they agree on 98.7\% of pixels. The gap between the two figures is dominated by a thin band at region boundaries rather than by disagreement about accessibility, visible as the red outlines in the disagreement panels of Fig.~\ref{fig:heatmaps}. Most of this band is the safety buffer the annotation deliberately draws around obstacles, a property of the protocol rather than a prediction error, and per setting 80--100\% of the pixels labelled only in the annotated map lie within three pixels of the predicted boundary. Discounting the band raises overall agreement to 97.9\%, and the three-pixel tolerance sits at the observed plateau of the similarity-versus-tolerance curve.

At the level of staged conditions, 58 of the 60 conducted conditions were predicted correctly, a 96.7\% match rate with a Wilson 95\% confidence interval of 88.6--99.1\%, split 26 of 27 for the Movilo and 32 of 33 for the GO2, and both misses involved small objects that the point-cloud noise filtering removes. At the level of the index, the predicted RII spans 80.9\% to 98.7\% across the settings and exceeded the value computed from the annotated map in every case, by 1.4 to 8.1 percentage points and 4.6 pooled (Table~\ref{tab:similarity}), and part of this offset is definitional, since the annotation counts its safety buffer as unreachable, so these figures bound the index error from above. Because the accessible class dominates every map, we also report chance-corrected agreement, and pooled Cohen's kappa is 0.62, a level depressed by class imbalance even though the residual disagreement is confined to the boundary band, while the accessible-class intersection over union of 95--98\% and accessible-label precision of 96.4--99.9\% show the labels are recovered reliably. The residual errors are asymmetric, with area wrongly marked accessible totalling 21.5\,m$^2$ across the five settings, eleven times the area wrongly marked unreachable, which is acceptable for a site-preparation screening tool, since small objects at deployment time remain the responsibility of onboard perception and the upper-bound reading makes the residual optimism explicit.

\subsection{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
Taken together, the experiments answer the three questions in order. Across the five settings the predicted maps matched the annotated behaviour on 92.6\% of the labelled area and on 58 of 60 conducted conditions, so the accessibility maps can be read as faithful forecasts of where a platform will and will not operate.

The fourteen route-level discrepancies trace to three mechanisms, namely the filtering of small floor-level objects from the cloud, footprint clearance not being enforced between pairs of obstacles, and negative obstacles such as floor depressions, which the current floor-slab analysis does not flag. Each maps to a concrete pipeline improvement, platform-aware filtering thresholds, footprint enforcement between obstacle pairs, and negative-obstacle detection from the ground plane. The direction of the first mechanism is platform-dependent, optimistic for the Movilo and conservative for the GO2, and that the errors follow each platform's own capabilities supports assessing a fleet from a single scan while showing that footprint and climbing tolerances alone do not fully capture how a quadruped robot negotiates small obstacles. The consistently optimistic index bias argues for reading a reported RII as an upper bound on deployment readiness.

The adjustment tests show that the diagnosed barriers are actionable, since relocating movable obstacles raised the index in every area by 15.6 to 52.1 percentage points, and the near-identical response of the two platforms assessed on the same scans indicates that one round of site preparation can serve several robots at once.

Three limitations remain. Each assessment is a snapshot of a changing site, and all settings come from a single project, with four relocation tests forming a small sample. The adjusted areas were verified by rescans but not yet re-walked by the physical robots, and the gains predicted in the relocation interface have not been compared with the measured ones, while the reference-footprint separation of~\eqref{eq:decomp} was likewise not exercised in this study. Broader validation across sites and larger intervention studies remain for future work.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
This paper introduced the Robot Inclusivity Index, a framework that turns a single point-cloud walkthrough of a construction site into a deployment assessment for a mobile robot, producing a robot-specific traversable map, an accessibility map, a single-figure index, and a ranked list of site changes. In a field study at an active construction site, the accessibility maps predicted for the Movilo and the GO2 matched the behaviour annotated from deployment video on 92.6\% of the labelled area, rising to 97.9\% once annotation boundary effects are discounted, and 58 of 60 test conditions were predicted correctly. Physically adjusting four further cluttered areas guided by the framework's barrier diagnosis raised their indices from as low as 44\% to above 93\%, a mean gain of 37 percentage points measured by repeat scans. Future work will verify the adjusted areas through robot re-deployment, extend validation to additional sites and larger intervention studies, and broaden the index beyond accessibility to the other dimensions of robot inclusivity.
